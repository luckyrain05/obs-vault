- These readings come from the ==Wages for Housework== movement of the 1970s, rooted in Italian autonomist Marxism.
- The central claim across all three texts: women's unwaged domestic labor is not outside capitalism — it is the hidden foundation that makes capitalism function.
- All three authors build on [[marxist theory]] but argue Marx and the broader Marxist tradition failed to account for the role of domestic and reproductive labor in producing surplus value.

# Reproductive Labor

**Reproductive labor**

- The work required to maintain and reproduce the workforce itself — cooking, cleaning, childcare, emotional support, sexual labor.
- Not "reproductive" in the biological sense alone. It refers to everything that keeps workers alive, functional, and returning to work each day.
- Marx's framework accounts for labor-power as a commodity the worker sells. But labor-power does not reproduce itself — someone has to cook the meals, wash the clothes, raise the next generation of workers.
- That someone is overwhelmingly women, and that labor is unwaged.
- The wage relation hides a second layer of exploitation — the capitalist pays the (male) worker a wage, and that wage is implicitly expected to cover the domestic labor his wife performs for free. The capitalist gets two workers for the price of one.

# Wages Against Housework (Federici, 1975)

- Federici's argument is not primarily that women should be paid for housework. The demand for wages is a ==political strategy==.
- Housework has been naturalized — treated as something women do out of love, instinct, or biological destiny, not as work.
	- By calling it unwaged labor and demanding compensation, the demand forces society to recognize it as work.
	- The demand ==denaturalizes== domestic labor. It strips away the idea that women are "naturally" suited to it.
- As long as housework is unwaged, it is invisible in economic analysis. It does not appear in GDP. It is not counted as productive labor. Women who perform it are classified as "non-working."
- The wage is not the end goal — it is a tool to expose the relationship. Once domestic labor is visible as labor, it can be refused, reorganized, or collectivized.
- Federici argues that women's liberation cannot come through simply entering the waged workforce (the liberal feminist position). Entering the factory does not abolish housework — it means women now perform two jobs.
- ==Refusal== is the political act. Refusing to perform unwaged labor disrupts the entire system of capitalist reproduction.

# Why Sexuality Is Work (Federici, 1975)

- Federici extends the analysis beyond housework to ==sexual and emotional labor==.
- Women are expected to provide sexual availability, emotional comfort, and psychological stability to male workers — all unwaged.
- This is not natural or voluntary. Sexuality has been ==disciplined== by capitalism:
	- Women's sexuality is shaped to serve men's needs, not their own.
	- The "good wife" performs emotional maintenance — listening, soothing, validating — that restores the male worker's capacity to return to the workplace.
- This labor is productive in the Marxist sense: it reproduces labor-power. A demoralized, emotionally broken worker cannot produce surplus value. The wife's emotional labor keeps him functional.
- Federici argues that the commodification of sexuality (prostitution, pornography) is not a deviation from this system but its logical extension — it makes explicit what marriage already does implicitly.
- The point is not moralistic. Federici is not condemning sex work — she is arguing that all women's sexual and emotional labor under capitalism is already work, whether inside marriage or outside it.

# Women and the Subversion of the Community (Dalla Costa & James, 1972)

- Dalla Costa and James make the most direct theoretical intervention: housework produces value.
- In orthodox Marxism, only waged labor in the factory produces surplus value. Domestic labor is considered "unproductive" — necessary but outside the circuit of capital.
- Dalla Costa rejects this. Housework does not just maintain the worker — it produces the commodity labor-power itself.
	- Without domestic labor, there is no labor-power to sell. No cooked meals, no clean clothes, no raised children → no workers → no surplus value → no capitalism.
	- Housework is not outside production. The home is a factory, and the housewife is a worker.
- If women are workers, then women are working class — not dependents of working-class men, but a class in their own right with their own relationship to capital.
- This redefines who the proletariat is. The factory worker is not the only revolutionary subject. The housewife, performing unwaged labor that capital depends on, is equally positioned to disrupt the system.
- ==Subversion== comes from organizing around unwaged work:
	- If women refuse domestic labor or demand wages for it, they attack capitalism at its reproductive base — not just at the point of production (the factory) but at the point of reproduction (the home).
	- This is a broader revolutionary strategy than traditional Marxism offered, which focused almost exclusively on the waged workplace.

# The Shared Framework

- The demand for wages is not reformist — it is a strategy to make the invisible visible, to transform a "natural duty" into a recognized labor relation, and to open it up to political contestation.
